About

I hold a PhD in Marine Sciences from CICIMAR-IPN in La Paz, Mexico (2026), and came to oceanography from engineering: I trained as an automation engineer and spent five years writing firmware, Linux drivers and embedded systems.

Since my MSc I have worked on pelagic Sargassum, the floating brown algae that have bloomed across the tropical Atlantic since 2011 and strand on Caribbean coasts every summer. I build detection algorithms for it across sensors, from Aqua-MODIS to Landsat 8/9 OLI, Sentinel-2 MSI and Sentinel-3 OLCI, and I calibrate Lagrangian drift models against GPS drifters and consecutive satellite images. I also set up coastal ocean models on unstructured meshes to resolve the channels and tides that global models miss. I care about honest validation: testing on bloom events and drifters the model has never seen.

In 2024-2025 I trained at the Nippon Foundation-POGO Centre of Excellence in Observational Oceanography in Canada, where I worked on kelp-canopy dynamics with the Hakai Institute and assessed Mexico's ocean-observing data for the Global Ocean Observing System.

Multi-sensor Sargassum detection and fractional cover

Published
Landsat 8/9 OLI
Sentinel-2 MSI
2025

A machine and deep learning framework on harmonised Landsat and Sentinel-2 imagery: Landsat reflectance mapped to Sentinel-2 with the HLS coefficients, a spectral library labelled in QGIS from 34 Level-2 scenes, and five classifiers (random forest, k-nearest neighbours, XGBoost, MLP, 1D CNN). Classifier probabilities serve as a sub-pixel fractional-cover proxy, and agreement between the five models flags uncertain detections.

Sargassum in Sentinel-3 OLCI full-resolution imagery

In preparation
Sentinel-3 OLCI, 300 m
Sentinel-2 MSI check
2026

At 300 m a Sargassum pixel is mostly water, so index thresholds learn their own false alarms. I labelled the algae by the shape of its anomaly against local water (red edge, NIR plateau, no blue excess), added look-alikes as hard negatives, and trained XGBoost, a 1D CNN and a residual U-Net in PyTorch. TreeSHAP, permutation and forward selection then showed that three bands carry the detection.

Calibrating drift from consecutive satellite images

In preparation
Sentinel-3 OLCI
6 current products
2026

Mats break up and re-form between daily overpasses, so matching the same aggregation twice is unreliable. Instead I advect the whole detection field of one image and score how well it lands on the next, discarding particles that end under the second image's clouds and comparing every configuration with a stationary forecast on the same particles. A GPU particle integrator, checked against OpenDrift, makes the search affordable.

94 overpass pairs, 7 Caribbean events (2018 to 2025)1,989 drift configurationsseparation 6.7 → 5.4 km

Drift forecasts against drifters on real rafts

Submitted, JMSE
OpenDrift
OceanParcels
2024 to 2026

Twelve GPS drifters placed in Sargassum rafts during the NIOZ Weeds of Change expedition (RV Pelagia, 2024) let me separate three sources of forecast error: the tracking framework, the drift parameters and the surface-current product. Under identical forcing, OpenDrift and OceanParcels agree; the current product matters far more. In my thesis, a gradient-boosting correction of the model velocities also raised skill on withheld drifters.

frameworks: +0.630 vs +0.626observation-based current with Ekman: +0.76 (+0.69 withheld)thesis ML correction: 0.53 → 0.75 out-of-sample

What drives the blooms, and can we see them coming?

In preparation
GLORYS12, PISCES, ERA5
2010 to 2022

A monthly data cube at 0.25° of ocean physics, nutrients, winds and climate indices, divided into ten Gaussian-mixture regions from source to receiving areas. Mann-Kendall trends and Granger causality show that nutrients lead Sargassum biomass by two to four months, the basis for a proposed seasonal index of bloom risk.
